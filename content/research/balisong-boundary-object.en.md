## Abstract

Why can one transformable object become local craft heritage, an instance of mechanical aesthetics, a performance medium, and a legal controversy? This article follows the balisong across workshops, heritage institutions, visual and interaction design, participatory media, statutes, and courts. It combines material-culture and object-biography methods with source criticism, bounded visual analysis, and comparative legal-text reading. The evidence supports neither a single certain origin story nor a design-determinist account. Instead, the article argues that the object’s visible transformation is an enabling affordance: it makes change publicly legible, while communities and institutions assign that change different values. In Batangas, later records connect the object to livelihood, place, and cultural representation; in design discourse, dynamic form can become perceptible aesthetic experience; in media, observable competence can circulate as performance; and in law, movement can become classificatory evidence. These domains do not form an uninterrupted tradition. Their connections are mediated, contested, and unevenly documented. The principal limitation is the scarcity of accessible maker-centered ethnography and early local archives.

**Keywords:** balisong; material culture; craft heritage; kinetic design; participatory culture; boundary object; legal classification

> **Working thesis — Interpretive.** The balisong’s paired-handle architecture did not predetermine its cultural history. It made transformation unusually visible. That visibility became an enabling affordance whose meanings were produced by craft relations, institutions, markets, media, audiences, and law.

## 1. Introduction: One Object, Four Social Lives

The historical record does not present the balisong in one stable frame. A Philippine education report published in 1951 placed its production among Batangas industries; a cultural encyclopedia later treated it within Philippine metalcraft; a United States administrative notice in 1971 treated imported examples as a classification problem; and later courts disagreed about the legal categories into which related objects fell.[^1] These records do not merely offer different opinions about an otherwise fixed thing. They make different attributes relevant. The economic report sees labor and local industry. The heritage source sees place and craft. The administrative and judicial sources see a mechanism described by legal language. Contemporary photographs and media, meanwhile, foreground a change of configuration that can be seen by an audience. The same recognizable object consequently acquires four social lives: locally situated craft, kinetic interface, performance medium, and disputed legal category.

This article asks not simply what a balisong is, but how its visible transformation has been made to mean different things. That distinction matters because familiar introductions often collapse history into an origin anecdote or reduce design to a named mechanism. The accessible record is not strong enough to prove a single, uncontested birthplace narrative. It is, however, strong enough to show that the word appeared in Philippine popular print by 1947, that production was reported as a Batangas industry by 1951, and that later Philippine institutions repeatedly attached the object to Taal, Batangas, and regional craft identity.[^2] Legal documents then show that classification did not follow automatically from appearance. It emerged from the wording of statutes, administrative interpretation, litigation, and imagined use.[^3]

The article’s central proposition is that transformation is publicly legible. With many designed objects, operation disappears into a casing or appears only as an outcome. Here, a change in configuration is exposed to the viewer. Design scholarship provides a vocabulary for understanding why behavior and dynamic form can enter aesthetic experience, but it does not prove that any particular movement must become culturally important.[^4] Affordance scholarship further warns that possibilities for action are relational: they depend on abilities, learned attention, conventions, and a form of life rather than residing in geometry alone.[^5] Visible movement is therefore not a sufficient cause. It is an enabling condition that different social worlds can recruit.

That qualification changes the thesis from a mechanical explanation into a cultural one. In a workshop or family economy, the object can condense knowledge, labor, reputation, and place. In design discourse, alteration of form can become part of what is perceived. In mediated participation, an observable act can be recorded, compared, discussed, and made to stand for competence. In law, the same motion can be translated into a statutory test or evidentiary description. The transition between these settings is not seamless. A local craft tradition does not automatically become an online performance culture; a design property does not dictate a court’s holding; and an institutional declaration of heritage does not simply preserve an untouched past. Each transition requires people, organizations, media formats, and classificatory work.

The argument proceeds by following the object across those settings. Section 2 defines an object-biography method and distinguishes documented fact from institutional framing and authorial interpretation. Section 3 reconstructs a cautious Batangas-centered history from dated texts and later heritage claims. Section 4 explains why dynamic form may become aesthetically perceptible without turning that observation into engineering analysis. Section 5 examines how visible skill is reformatted by participatory media while refusing an unproven continuity with workshop transmission. Section 6 reads statutes and cases as sites where movement becomes classification. Sections 7 and 8 synthesize the object as a qualified boundary object and test counterarguments. The conclusion asks what social categories move when the object moves.

## 2. Method: Following the Object Across Social Worlds

Material-culture history begins from a simple methodological refusal: objects are not illustrations placed beside the “real” history contained in text. Serena Dyer describes material culture as an interdisciplinary field concerned with objects, meanings, production, exchange, use, and the different literacies required to interpret them.[^6] That approach is especially useful for a transformable object whose public identities are scattered among reports, photographs, institutional exhibitions, media, and law. Yet it also raises a danger. Because an object seems tangible, researchers can mistake their own visual confidence for historical knowledge. The present study therefore does not infer real scale, internal structure, chronology, or provenance from appearance.

Object biography offers a way to organize heterogeneous evidence without inventing a continuous origin story. Appadurai’s “social life” approach and Kopytoff’s cultural-biography chapter redirect analysis toward circulation and changing regimes of value.[^7] Gosden and Marshall sharpen the point: meanings are invested through social interaction and renegotiated over an object’s life; physical alteration is not required for social meaning to change.[^8] “Biography” is a research strategy, not a claim that the object possesses a human life or autonomous intention. It asks when the same object type is named, exchanged, displayed, evaluated, or regulated differently, and who gains the authority to make each classification.

The related concept of a boundary object is used here in a deliberately extended sense. Star and Griesemer developed it to explain cooperation among social worlds whose participants did not share a complete consensus. Boundary objects were adaptable to different viewpoints while sufficiently robust to retain identity across them.[^9] Craft workers, cultural institutions, online audiences, and courts do not constitute the coordinated scientific network in their original case. They often neither cooperate nor agree. The concept remains useful, however, if it names the minimum recognizability that allows divergent groups to argue about “the same” thing. Its use must not erase unequal power: a legal classification can constrain other meanings, and institutional heritage language can eclipse maker testimony.

Five evidence states govern the analysis. **Directly documented** means that a source explicitly records an event, term, institutional decision, or claim within the limits of its genre. **Visually observed** means only that a feature is visible in a rights-cleared image; it does not license dimensional or mechanical inference. **Institutionally framed** identifies what a museum, university, municipality, cultural body, or government says the object represents. **Historically contested** identifies incompatible narratives or classifications. **Authorial interpretation** marks the article’s synthesis, using terms such as “suggests,” “may help explain,” or “can be understood as.” These states keep a 1947 fictional usage from becoming design evidence and keep a contemporary municipal identity statement from becoming an early origin proof.

The source base was assembled before the prose. Fifty candidates were logged across Philippine repositories, universities, cultural organizations, official legal databases, museum catalogues, and scholarship. Thirty-four were available as full public text, five as verified partial text, six only as abstracts, and five only as metadata. Fifteen lawful research copies were downloaded and hashed locally; the files remain outside the public repository. Abstract-only sources are cited only for what their institutional abstracts state. Metadata-only records provide discovery or chronology, not substantive proof. Commercial pages, forums, social media posts, instructional videos, anonymous histories, and mutually copied origin narratives were excluded from evidentiary use.

The uneven archive is itself a finding. The most directly relevant local thesis located in the De La Salle University repository is accessible only through its abstract. That abstract reports a qualitative inquiry into life histories, family transmission, livelihood, and cultural symbolism, but the article cannot verify its underlying interviews or page-level argument.[^10] A 1969 Nayong Pilipino brochure has institutional catalogue metadata and a later scholarly citation chain, but the original was not acquired. This study therefore cites the later thesis as later analysis and does not claim to have read the brochure. Such distinctions prevent the appearance of an abundant web bibliography from masking scarce primary access.

Visual analysis was bounded in the same way. Two licensed contemporary photographs by the same creator are treated as a matched record of visible configurations, not two independent sources and not a historical typology. A police-museum photograph is evidence of display context, not provenance. No sequential performance media appear in the article. No image is used to calculate dimensions, infer an internal mechanism, fill an occluded area, or reconstruct manufacture. The article’s figures include original evidence diagrams labeled as interpretive visualizations, so they cannot be mistaken for historical photographs.

The legal method compares texts rather than advising users. Statutes, administrative notices, opinions, and reported decisions are separated by jurisdiction, date, and procedural role. A court’s interpretation is not treated as a universal definition; a dissent is not a holding; an attorney general’s opinion is not an appellate judgment. Legal sources were last checked on 1 September 2026, with the federal code page indicating laws in effect through 29 August 2026. The legal discussion is historical and conceptual and does not address what any person may possess, purchase, transport, or use.

## 3. From Workshop to Place: Craft, Kinship, Livelihood, and Batangueño Identity

A cautious local history begins with dated traces rather than a definitive invention story. In the 12 October 1947 issue of the Filipino periodical *Ilang-Ilang*, the word “balisong” appears in popular fiction without an explanatory aside. Fiction cannot establish the object’s external form, production place, or origin, but it can show that the term was sufficiently intelligible within that publication’s imagined readership.[^11] Four years later, Gregorio Lardizabal’s report on community-centered schools, written after provincial visits, included balisong production among Batangas industries and noted a division of labor. That is direct evidence for organized economic activity in the province by 1951, not proof that the craft began there or that one settlement held a monopoly.[^12]

These two sources are valuable partly because their purposes differ. The periodical uses the word narratively; the education report inventories community development. Neither is a retrospective heritage campaign. Their independence supports a limited chronological statement: both vocabulary and an associated Batangas industry were publicly documented by the early 1950s. They do not support the stronger claim that every later design descends from one workshop lineage. They also cannot settle competing etymologies or claims of precedence, which are often repeated online without an acquired early source.

Later Philippine institutions make the place-object relation far more explicit. The 1994 *CCP Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* situates the object within metalcraft, describes it as associated with Batangas, and connects production with places in Taal. Its genre matters: an encyclopedia organizes varied local practices into a national cultural reference work, selecting an object as representative folk art and local technology.[^13] The entry provides a strong record of institutional framing in 1994, but its technical content was excluded and the accessible extract does not expose a full source genealogy. The framing is evidence of how a national cultural institution classified the object, not an unmediated voice of every maker.

Mary Vincelle Yasa’s study of Nayong Pilipino adds an earlier institutional itinerary. She examines a 1969 brochure that organized the park through regional craft displays, including a Batangas-and-balisong association, and interprets the site as both an aesthetic project and a nationalizing marketplace.[^14] Because the original brochure was not locally acquired, the article does not quote it as a primary source. What can be said is narrower: Yasa’s verified pages document a later scholar’s reading and bibliographic trail, showing that a national tourism institution had made the association legible by 1969. The episode is important because the object moves here from local industry into staged regional representation. Craft becomes an exhibit through which a national public is invited to recognize place.

Contemporary university and municipal sources extend that itinerary into “living craft” discourse. A 2024 University of Batangas event, *Likhalina*, paired burda and balisong within a public program about Batangas heritage, invited a local artisan, and connected craft to livelihood and adaptation.[^15] The Municipality of Taal’s page calls the object Taal’s pride, while its El Pasubat festival groups local products and performance under a civic and tourism identity.[^16] These pages are not neutral ethnographies. They are events of heritage-making: the university curates a demonstration and educational encounter; the municipality converts place association into public identity and visitor address.

Family and kinship appear most directly in the abstract of Daniel John Felix Galvan’s 2016 De La Salle thesis, which reports life-story research about cultural symbolism, livelihood, and transmission in Barangay Balisong.[^17] Because only the abstract was available, these themes remain provisional rather than fully verified findings in this article. The University of Batangas page corroborates that contemporary heritage programming presents named artisans and intergenerational continuity, but an event report cannot substitute for sustained maker-centered ethnography. The evidence therefore suggests that family relations and livelihood matter to local cultural significance; it does not quantify their prevalence or establish an unbroken genealogy.

UNESCO’s general framework clarifies why the distinction matters. Its guidance on traditional craftsmanship emphasizes knowledge and skill, transmission, and viable social conditions rather than treating the finished object alone as heritage. Its broader explanation of intangible cultural heritage stresses that communities recognize, transmit, and recreate practices over time.[^18] Neither page designates balisong craft as UNESCO heritage. Instead, the framework helps identify what would need to be documented: who recognizes the practice, how knowledge is transmitted, what conditions support practitioners, and how contemporary change is negotiated.

Heritage language should consequently be analyzed, not merely repeated. Laurajane Smith’s account of authorized heritage discourse shows how professional and institutional categories can privilege expert values and material monuments over community meanings.[^19] Patricia Gonzalez’s Philippine study of pabalat-wrapped pastillas, available here only through its institutional abstract, makes a related point in a different craft domain: community participation, tourism demonstrations, festivals, training, museums, memory, and ownership do not align automatically.[^20] Applied cautiously, these works suggest that calling balisong a “living tradition” may protect recognition while also selecting representatives, performances, and narratives suitable for institutions.

This double action is visible in the local sources. El Pasubat makes a civic economy out of a list of recognizable products; Nayong Pilipino translated a regional craft into a national display; the university event translated maker knowledge into a scheduled educational program.[^21] None of this renders heritage false. It shows that heritage is active work. A craft can carry memory and livelihood precisely because people keep reorganizing its public meaning. At the same time, tourism and promotional frames may favor spectacular or marketable features over less visible labor, disagreement, or changing economic conditions.

Place-product language also invites a legal-institutional comparison. IPOPHL describes geographical indications as signs linking products to geographical origin and associated qualities, reputation, or characteristics.[^22] This general definition demonstrates one formal way that place and product can be stabilized. The research did not verify a balisong geographical-indication registration, so the page cannot be cited as if one exists. Its relevance is analytical: it shows that apparently natural place associations depend on documentary, collective, and institutional procedures.

The available evidence thus supports a limited but meaningful conclusion. By the mid-twentieth century, the term circulated in Philippine print and a Batangas industry was documented. By 1969 and 1994, national display and reference institutions framed the object as Batangas-associated craft. Contemporary local institutions continue to present it through heritage, livelihood, and identity. The stronger origin story remains contested, and maker-centered evidence remains insufficient. “Batangueño identity” in this article names a documented local and institutional attachment; it does not reduce the diverse cultures of Batangas, much less the Philippines, to a single object.

## 4. Motion Made Visible: The Mechanical Aesthetics of Transformation

The balisong presents a design-historical problem because a static outline does not exhaust its public form. What observers recognize is not only a configuration but a transition between configurations. The article calls this **visible transformation**: change appears at the object’s exterior and can be perceived as an event. The phrase is intentionally nontechnical. It does not describe internal engineering, specify joints, calculate angles, or reproduce a sequence. It identifies a phenomenological fact supported by paired contemporary photographs: viewers can distinguish more than one public state of the same object.[^23]

Design aesthetics provides a vocabulary for this difference between looking and encountering. Paul Hekkert limits aesthetic experience to sensory pleasure and explains preferences through principles such as unity in variety, while also treating product experience as broader than visual styling.[^24] Philip Ross and Stephan Wensveen argue more directly that interactive behavior can have “dynamic form” and that aesthetic interaction includes practical, social, ethical, bodily, cognitive, and emotional dimensions.[^25] Their cases concern interactive products rather than historical edged objects, so the transfer is interpretive. Still, they make it possible to say that behavior may be part of form rather than a function hidden behind it.

[[figure:F02]]

Several perceptual qualities follow from that proposition. Paired external forms can generate an impression of symmetry; concealment and unfolding make absence and appearance temporally related; repetition can produce rhythm; and visible change provides feedback to both participant and observer. These are descriptive categories, not claims about exact geometry. They help explain why a transformed state may feel intelligible as the completion of an event rather than merely a second photograph. Hekkert’s “unity in variety” and Ross and Wensveen’s dynamic form support the broader reasoning, but neither source proves that all audiences experience the balisong identically.[^26]

The observer is central. A mechanical watch can expose moving parts while leaving most interaction to contemplation. A folding fan couples an expanding surface to gesture and social convention. Transformable furniture makes reconfiguration serve spatial economy; a camera may turn adjustment into a visible ritual; a stage prop can make change carry narrative meaning. These comparisons show that motion is not culturally self-executing. Objects with visible mechanisms take different social paths because settings teach people what to notice, who may act, what counts as competence, and whether transformation is practical, decorative, ceremonial, playful, or suspect.

Affordance theory prevents the design account from becoming deterministic. Rietveld and Kiverstein define a rich landscape of affordances in relation to abilities available within a form of life.[^27] Van Dijk and Rietveld foreground sociomaterial practice, situating perception and coordination within learned patterns rather than reducing them to an isolated object property.[^28] The implication for this study is decisive: the paired-handle architecture does not carry craft heritage, performance culture, or legal controversy inside itself. It affords different engagements to differently skilled and institutionally positioned people.

Ramstead, Veissière, and Kirmayer extend this relational approach through “cultural affordances,” distinguishing possibilities that depend on shared expectations, norms, conventions, and coordinated attention.[^29] Their framework helps explain why the same visible event can solicit incompatible readings. A craft institution may direct attention toward transmission and place. A design observer may attend to symmetry and temporal form. A peer audience may attend to recognizable competence. A statute may make the mode of transformation legally salient. The object has not changed between these descriptions; the regime of attention has.

This framework also places ethics inside aesthetics rather than after it. Ross and Wensveen explicitly connect aesthetic interaction to social and ethical consequences.[^30] Analyzing dynamic form does not require celebrating every use or neutralizing legal concern. It requires describing how perceptible behavior influences response. In the balisong’s case, public legibility makes transformation available for admiration, instruction, anxiety, classification, or institutional framing. The article intentionally stops before instruction: it does not name gestures, provide stages, or convert analysis into a repeatable sequence.

“Mechanical aesthetics” therefore means the aesthetic legibility of transformation, not the beauty of a technical specification. The function becomes perceptible because change is presented at the surface as an event. Yet perceptibility is only potential. Communities stabilize what should be seen in it. The next section follows one such stabilization: the conversion of visible competence into mediated performance and social identity.

## 5. When Skill Becomes Spectacle: Performance, Participation, and Social Identity

A visible transformation creates the possibility of an audience, but not every audience forms a culture. The distinction is crucial. A recording can circulate as novelty without sustaining shared standards; a group can exchange images without transmitting knowledge; a platform metric can reward attention without producing durable belonging. This article therefore uses participatory-culture theory as an interpretive framework, not as proof that every online balisong scene is a coherent community of practice.

Jenkins and his coauthors define participatory culture through relatively low barriers to expression, support for creating and sharing, informal mentorship, a sense that contributions matter, and social connection. They also distinguish participation, a cultural property, from interactivity, a technological one.[^31] That distinction parallels the argument developed here. Visible transformation may make recording and comparison possible, but media practices turn possibility into circulation. Posts, comments, remixes, reputation signals, and archives decide which performances become legible and which disappear.

The concept of a community of practice adds a needed account of competence and identity. Farnsworth, Kleanthous, and Wenger-Trayner describe participation, regimes of competence, power, boundaries, and identity as intertwined rather than as a simple transfer of information.[^32] Cox’s comparative review warns that the term has shifted across different theoretical uses and that direct, situated practice should not be confused with every loose association.[^33] Applied to visible object performance, these sources suggest that reputation depends on negotiated standards: audiences learn what to recognize, practitioners imagine possible identities, and boundaries form around who can credibly evaluate whom.

Nothing in that analysis requires reproducing an action. It concerns the social visibility of skill. When an outcome can be watched and compared, a bodily accomplishment becomes communicative: it can signal commitment, affiliation, style, or mastery to an informed audience. Evaluation then feeds back into identity. The same event may be read by insiders as competence, by a platform as engaging content, by a cultural institution as an inappropriate reduction of craft, and by regulators as evidence of imagined use. Performance is not an intrinsic layer of the object; it is an audience relation built around an observable change.

Media circulation alters scale and provenance. Jenkins’s ecological approach emphasizes that technologies operate within cultural, legal, political, and economic institutions and that the same technology can support different ends.[^34] Once the balisong’s transformation enters networked media, a Batangas-associated craft can circulate without its place history. Short formats favor immediately legible action; thumbnails and loops make repeated change more visible than workshop relations or archival uncertainty. Commercial actors can further detach an aesthetic from a local biography, while algorithms may amplify what produces reaction. This study did not conduct a platform dataset analysis, so those mechanisms remain plausible interpretations rather than measured findings.

The historical relation between local craft and global performance must therefore remain open. The 1951 report documents industry, not contemporary performance. The 1969 display places craft in a national tourist frame, not an online peer network. Present institutional pages combine artisanship, livelihood, and public demonstration, while general participatory-culture research explains how mediated groups can organize learning and recognition.[^35] None of these sources establishes an unbroken transmission line from workshop practice to internet performance. Continuity, rupture, and reinvention are competing hypotheses.

That gap is analytically productive. If later performance is partly a reinvention, then visible transformation functions as a bridge without guaranteeing historical continuity. A public action can be abstracted from local production, standardized by camera conventions, and reattached to new identities. Participants may know the object primarily through global media; local institutions may respond by emphasizing heritage and livelihood; legal institutions may see the same imagery as confirmation of risk. The object biography branches rather than proceeds along one line.

The gap is also empirical. No peer-reviewed, maker- and participant-centered ethnography of contemporary balisong performance communities was located in the reviewed corpus. Existing licensed media records in Balisong Atlas are self-published and unsuitable for strong historical or sociological claims. Their sequences were excluded from this article because showing them could turn visual-cultural analysis into operational content. Future research should instead seek interviews, platform governance records, content-moderation policies, and ethically sampled non-instructional media. Until then, claims about peer learning, reputation, and identity are theory-guided interpretations with explicit limits.

Performance culture consequently reveals both the power and incompleteness of visible transformation. It makes competence observable enough to invite comparison, but standards come from communities. It makes action recordable enough to circulate, but distribution comes from platforms and commercial systems. It makes identity expressible, but recognition depends on audiences. The movement is a medium only because social relations make it carry a message.

## 6. Classification Under Motion: How Law Turns Design into a Dispute

> **Legal research notice.** This section analyzes legal classification historically and conceptually. It does not provide legal advice concerning possession, purchase, transport, or use. Legal sources were last verified on 1 September 2026.

Law offers the clearest evidence that visible transformation can become an institutional fact. Section 1241 of title 15 of the United States Code defines categories partly through specified modes of opening rather than through static silhouette alone.[^36] The point here is not to reproduce a technical test or tell readers how any current object will be treated. It is that statutory language selects change over time as relevant. Classification asks what an object is imagined to do, how that action is described, and whether the description fits words enacted for a legal purpose.

The 21 May 1971 Federal Register makes the resulting uncertainty unusually visible. In an official notice titled “Imported Balisong Knives,” Customs acknowledged a federal district-court decision involving particular merchandise, limited the decision’s reach to that merchandise, and maintained its broader administrative position while interpretation remained contested.[^37] The notice is important not because it supplies a timeless definition, but because it records an agency managing the boundary of a category. An object could be excluded, admitted under a judgment, or treated as outside that judgment depending on how institutional scope was drawn.

*Taylor v. United States* later placed the federal disagreement before the Sixth Circuit. The appellate court treated Customs’ reading as permissible under the statutory scheme and reversed the lower judgment that had favored the importer.[^38] A contemporaneous Department of Justice bulletin reported the decision within federal enforcement practice.[^39] These two sources are related rather than independent: the bulletin summarizes the case from an executive-branch perspective. The judicial opinion remains the primary authority for its holding; the bulletin shows how the result circulated institutionally.

California’s path demonstrates a different sequence of institutional translation. Attorney General Opinion 85-902 analyzed whether the object fell within then-existing state language, offering an official interpretation before appellate adjudication.[^40] *People v. Quattrone* subsequently held that butterfly knives were included under the statute at issue there.[^41] Neither document should be lifted into a current compliance guide. Their relevance is historical: executive legal analysis and judicial interpretation took visible transformation and translated it into terms such as type, operation, and statutory inclusion.

The disagreement becomes internal in *State v. Riddall*. The New Mexico Court of Appeals majority concluded that the object fell within the state definition before it; the dissent argued for a different reading of the category.[^42] Because the majority and dissent confronted the same record, their divergence cannot be explained by two visibly different artifacts. It arose from interpretive choices about statutory language, ordinary meaning, legislative purpose, and category boundaries. The decision makes legal classification appear not as discovery but as argument.

*State v. Strange* supplies a comparative counterpoint. The Alaska Court of Appeals declined to include the object within undefined statutory terms before that court.[^43] Placed beside *Riddall* and *Quattrone*, the result does not show that one court understood the “true” object and another failed. The cases interpreted different texts in different jurisdictions and procedural settings. What remains comparable is the legal operation: each court converted a dynamic object into a verbal category, then decided whether the fit was adequate.

These sources expose three layers often collapsed in popular legal summaries. A **statute** supplies enacted words. An **administrative interpretation** applies or enforces those words within delegated authority. A **judicial interpretation** reviews a dispute and produces a holding with a particular procedural reach. Present legal status adds a fourth layer because texts may later be amended, superseded, or narrowed. This article freezes none of those layers into advice. Its as-of date records when the source check occurred; it does not promise completeness across local law or later developments.

The Philippine case *People v. Lasanas* illustrates another evidentiary limit. The Supreme Court opinion reproduces historical legal language in which “balisong” appears as a named category.[^44] That fact supports a claim about legal vocabulary in a particular historical context. It does not establish design origin, regional craft history, or present classification. The case’s other facts are not needed for this argument and are omitted. Legal sources must not be recruited to answer questions they did not decide.

[[figure:F03]]

Law therefore imagines more than a static object. It imagines a mechanism described in language, a potential user, a context of circulation, and a public purpose. Those imaginings may be grounded in evidence, but they are also shaped by statutory categories. Classification can then feed back into public image: museum displays, news labels, and platform policies may repeat the legal category, making the object’s regional craft biography less visible. Law does not merely discover risk already present in design; it constructs administrable boundaries around selected attributes.

This feedback should not be romanticized. Describing legal categorization as cultural work does not minimize the purposes of regulation or aestheticize harm. It identifies how design becomes evidence. The same visible transformation that design analysis treats as dynamic form can be treated by law as proof relevant to an enacted classification. The difference is not in the movement alone but in the institution, the question, and the consequence attached to it.

## 7. The Balisong as a Boundary Object

The four social lives can now be compared without pretending they form a single consensus. Local craft discourse foregrounds workshop knowledge, livelihood, family, place, and the authority to represent Batangas. Design discourse foregrounds perceptible change and the relation among object, participant, and observer. Participatory media foregrounds observable competence, circulation, feedback, and identity. Legal discourse foregrounds statutory wording, imagined action, and institutional consequence. Each social world selects an attribute that the others may ignore.

Star and Griesemer’s boundary object is robust enough to maintain identity and flexible enough to serve different viewpoints.[^45] The balisong fits that description only with qualifications. First, the relevant groups do not necessarily share a project. A municipality promoting heritage and a court interpreting a statute may never communicate. Second, adaptability is unequal. Legal categories can prohibit or stigmatize; platforms can amplify; national institutions can authorize a regional image; makers may have less documentary visibility than organizations speaking about them. Third, the object is not merely a neutral carrier. Its visible transformation helps make certain translations easier, even though it does not determine them.

Object biography contributes the temporal dimension. Gosden and Marshall argue that meanings arise and are renegotiated through social interaction, sometimes without physical change.[^46] The dated archive follows that process: popular vocabulary in 1947; a Batangas industry in 1951; a national display citation chain in 1969; federal administrative dispute in 1971; cultural encyclopedic framing in 1994; contemporary university and municipal heritage work. These are not successive improvements toward a final definition. They are episodes in which institutions attach different significance to a recognizable object.

Affordance theory contributes the relational dimension. Cultural affordances depend on shared expectations and regimes of attention, while sociomaterial practice shapes which possibilities are salient.[^47] Visible transformation functions as a common interface because it can be attended to as craft accomplishment, aesthetic event, public performance, or statutory mechanism. The interface is common; the evaluation is not. What an informed observer sees depends on learned concern and institutional location.

The object is therefore a **legal boundary object** in two senses. It sits at the boundary between named legal categories, and it exposes the boundary-making labor of officials and courts. It is a **heritage boundary object** because local, national, educational, and tourism institutions use the place-object association differently. It is a **media boundary object** because a visible act can circulate beyond the community that produced the material object. None of these labels cancels the others. Their friction is the historical phenomenon.

The central synthesis can now be stated carefully: the four identities are not accidental labels attached to an inert core. They are competing interpretations of the same publicly visible capacity for transformation.[^48] “Same” does not mean identical examples, unchanged design, or universal perception. It means that observers can recognize a family of objects through a transformable relation and then make that relation answer different questions. Heritage asks where knowledge belongs. Design asks how change is experienced. Performance asks how competence becomes visible. Law asks which language and consequence follow.

Architecture is not destiny. A folding fan, watch, camera, or transformable chair can also make movement visible without acquiring the same contested biography. Media genres, colonial and national institutions, local economies, commercial circulation, and legal regimes mediate what movement becomes. The balisong is analytically valuable because its social worlds make that mediation unusually explicit. One can watch classification happen around an observable change.

## 8. Counterarguments and Limits

### Structure is not a social cause

The first objection is that paired handles are simply a mechanical arrangement and that all cultural significance comes from outside design. The objection is correct if the thesis treats architecture as a sufficient cause. This article does not. Affordances are relational to abilities, practices, and forms of life; cultural affordances depend on shared expectations and attention.[^49] The structure matters because it makes a kind of transformation available and visible. Whether that potential becomes heritage, performance, or legal evidence is socially mediated. “Enabling affordance” preserves a role for form without turning form into fate.

A stronger version of the objection asks whether visible transformation is doing any explanatory work at all. The answer lies in cross-domain selection. Legal texts explicitly make opening action relevant; interaction-aesthetics theory explains how behavior can become dynamic form; participatory-culture theory explains how observable creation and performance circulate. These independent literatures repeatedly select change rather than static appearance. Their convergence does not prove a causal model, but it supports visible transformation as a productive comparative variable.

### Online performance may be a discontinuity

The second objection is that internet performance is recent and disconnected from local craft history. The available evidence cannot defeat that objection, and the paper should not try. The 1951 industry report and later heritage displays do not establish a lineage to online practice; participatory-media scholarship describes general cultural processes, not this object’s transmission.[^50] A future study might find continuity through families, migration, commerce, or media. It might instead find that global performance is a reinvention organized by platforms and brands. The responsible conclusion is that continuity is unproven.

This limit changes the language of the performance section. It does not say that a Batangas practice “became” internet performance through a continuous tradition. It says that a Batangas-associated object was reformatted within another social world, where visible movement could support new regimes of competence and identity. The distinction preserves the local craft history from being used as a decorative ancestry for a global scene.

### Legal controversy cannot be reduced to aesthetics

The third objection is that legal dispute arises because legislators and courts classify the object as a weapon, not because they appreciate or contest mechanical aesthetics. That objection also corrects an overextended thesis. Courts are not design critics, and regulatory purpose should not be aestheticized. Yet the primary texts show that opening mechanism enters statutory and judicial language.[^51] Dynamic form is therefore relevant as evidence, not as beauty. The bridge between design and law is the public legibility of change, while the values and consequences remain institutionally distinct.

The analysis also resists the inverse mistake: treating a legal category as the object’s complete social truth. A holding answers a legal question under a specific text and posture. It does not erase craft identity, settle origin, or dictate aesthetic experience. Different courts reached different results because legal categories are constructed through language and interpretation. That is precisely why the object can stand at a boundary without being naturally contained by one side.

### Heritage can be a contemporary construction

The fourth objection is that “local cultural heritage” may be a recent institutional or tourism brand. The source record partly supports this concern. Nayong Pilipino, the CCP encyclopedia, a municipal festival, and a university exhibition all curate the object for publics; Smith and Gonzalez show why authorized discourse, tourism, ownership, and community participation require scrutiny.[^52] Heritage institutions do not merely preserve identity. They select narrators, occasions, vocabulary, and visible signs.

But “constructed” does not mean invented from nothing. The 1947 and 1951 records predate the contemporary heritage pages and establish vocabulary and industry. Later institutions build on a documented association, even as they reshape it. The key research question becomes who is authorized to define the tradition now: workshop families, artisans, municipal organizers, national institutions, researchers, or commercial intermediaries. The present source base has more institutional speech than maker speech, so it cannot resolve that distribution of authority.

### The archive is linguistically and geographically uneven

The final limitation is the archive itself. English-language scholarship and digitized institutional pages are overrepresented. The 1947 Filipino text proves the value of vernacular sources but does not compensate for missing oral histories, family archives, local newspapers, workshop records, or unpublished research. The most relevant Filipino-language thesis is abstract-only. The original 1969 brochure is absent. Museum catalogue searches produced records for Philippine knives but no rights-cleared, provenance-rich historical balisong object suitable for visual comparison.[^53]

The legal corpus is also selective. It illustrates classificatory disagreement; it is not a comprehensive survey of every jurisdiction or later amendment. Platform evidence is weaker still: no systematic corpus, participant interviews, or moderation records were collected. Image rights narrowed visual choices to licensed contemporary context and original diagrams. These limits explain why the paper is a Research Draft. They are not gaps to be filled by AI-generated certainty.

## 9. Conclusion: What Moves When the Object Moves?

When the balisong moves, more than an object changes configuration. A relation between place and product can move from workshop economy into national display. Functional change can move into aesthetic perception when its temporal form becomes visible. Bodily competence can move into public performance when media and audiences make it comparable. Design can move into legal evidence when statutes and courts select mechanism as a classificatory attribute. None of those translations is automatic, and none is complete.

The evidence supports an object biography rather than a simple origin tale. It documents Philippine vocabulary in 1947, Batangas industry in 1951, later national and local heritage framing, and recurring legal disputes over classification. It does not establish a single certain birthplace, an uninterrupted line from local craft to online performance, or a universal response to dynamic form. Those limits are central findings because they reveal how repetition can masquerade as corroboration and how institutional visibility can stand in for community evidence.

Visible transformation is the article’s answer to the research question, but only in relational form. It is an enabling affordance: public enough to be noticed, repeatable enough to be mediated, and describable enough to enter law. Craft traditions teach one way of attending to it; design discourse another; performance communities and platforms another; legal institutions another.[^54] The architecture opens a field of interpretation. Social worlds decide which possibilities matter and what consequences follow.

This formulation avoids both technological determinism and cultural weightlessness. Form matters because some interactions are more publicly legible than others. Culture matters because legibility has no fixed meaning. Institutions matter because they can stabilize a meaning long enough to make it heritage, reputation, or law. Power matters because those stabilizations have unequal reach: a maker’s account, a municipal festival, a platform ranking, and an appellate holding do not speak with the same authority.

The balisong is analytically valuable not because it belongs naturally to any one category, but because its visible transformation repeatedly exposes the instability of the categories imposed upon it. That insight can travel to other dynamic objects. To understand a folding fan, transformable furnishing, mechanical display, camera, or stage prop, one should ask not only what moves, but who is authorized to name the movement, where it is watched, what history is attached to it, and which institution makes that interpretation consequential.

The next research step is not a more confident reconstruction. It is a better archive: maker-centered oral histories conducted with consent; Filipino-language and municipal records; the original Nayong Pilipino brochure; provenance-rich museum records; and non-instructional studies of media circulation. Such evidence may revise the thesis. An evidence-first object biography must remain open to that revision.
